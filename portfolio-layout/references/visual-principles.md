# Visual Principles

Use for information hierarchy, contrast, balance, visual focus, density, color hierarchy, three-column layout, and visual rhythm.

## Source Coverage

From Tip items 06, 08, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 20, and 26.

## Information Hierarchy: Bone Before Skin

The visual surface is the "皮像"; the information hierarchy is the "骨像". A beautiful page still fails if reviewers cannot quickly tell what matters.

Build hierarchy in this order:

1. Identify project/section/page message.
2. Divide content into title, section, key statement, evidence, caption, and supporting details.
3. Assign visual weight: size, thickness, position, color, spacing, and image scale.
4. Add visuals only after the information structure is readable.

## Text Hierarchy

- Project title: largest.
- Section title: second largest.
- Key label or insight: medium and more emphasized.
- Body: smallest and concise.
- Captions and notes: small but legible.

If everything is similar in size and weight, the page becomes flat even if the content is strong. Do not invent exact title/body size ratios unless the source material provides them.

## Contrast and Balance

Use four contrast types:

- **大小对比**: Combine large, medium, and small elements. Avoid only one huge element plus tiny scattered details.
- **疏密对比**: Let dense information areas breathe. Avoid a page that is all text or one side empty while the other is overloaded.
- **曲直对比**: Start with rectangular order, then add limited curves or irregular forms. Too many curves early can make the page chaotic.
- **图文对比**: Text must be concise and connected with images. Interweave text and visuals when it improves reading.

Balance does not mean symmetry. A page can be asymmetric and still stable if visual weight is distributed intentionally.

## Three-Column Layout

The three-column method is useful for fast, clear research or concept pages:

- Left: concept introduction, theory, or context.
- Middle: visual core, collage, mind map, or central logic.
- Right: support, references, evidence, or secondary explanation.

This structure works because each column has a clear function. Avoid making three columns all equally loud.

## Color and Attention Control

From item 14:

- Unify the project color tone so pages feel connected.
- Weaken background color when it makes the page dull or competes with content.
- Use repeated shapes, outlines, circles, lines, or highlights across pages to create cross-page continuity.
- Enlarge key visuals and suppress secondary information so the reader knows where to look first.
- "Advanced" feeling often comes from subtracting, not adding.

## Visual Rhythm for System Projects

From item 08:

- Let page rhythm match design-thinking rhythm.
- Use light backgrounds for problem definition and data analysis when a rational tone is needed.
- Shift to interface/system colors during design logic and system explanation.
- Use darker structure/scenario pages for final visual closure if the project supports it.

## Scan-Time Reality

From item 16:

- Reviewers may scan a project very quickly. Source item 16 states that if the title and main visual cannot communicate the logic in 5 seconds, patience may be lost, and that pages should allow people to catch the key point within 10 seconds.
- Compress early research into Key Insights.
- Make Development/Deliver visually substantial rather than hiding it after long research pages.

## Cognitive Structures for Visual Thinking

From item 12:

- **Circle**: whole, system, loop, ecology.
- **Line**: process, flow, path, timeline, cause-effect.
- **Network**: relationship, influence, stakeholders, multi-party system.

Choose visual forms by asking what the information cognitively resembles, not by picking a fashionable chart.
